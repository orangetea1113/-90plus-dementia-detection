Does matching feature distributions help speech-based models transfer between cohorts?

A model trained on conversations from the 90+ cohort, tested on I-CONECT: which speech features carry over, and which ones get in the way.

At a glance

Small studies often want to reuse a model trained on a larger one. We asked whether that works better if we keep only the speech features that look alike in both cohorts. It mostly does not. What matters is whether a feature points the same way in both cohorts, and a similar-looking distribution says nothing about that.

Three findings

  1. Similar-looking features are not better features. Keeping only the closest-matching features did not reliably improve transfer, and the strictest filters made it worse.
  2. A light clean-up helps a little. Dropping the 17 worst-shifted features (mostly recording-condition acoustic measures) raised one model's accuracy by up to 0.03, more than random feature sets of the same size, though not significantly more than using all features.
  3. What transfers is fluency and timing; what hurts is features that flip meaning. Shorter sentences, more pausing and turn-taking delay point the same way in both cohorts. The features that hurt are mostly acoustic measures whose link to MCI reverses between cohorts, which no distribution-similarity score can detect.

Practical takeaway. Remove only the few features that are clearly shifted between cohorts, and do not use distribution similarity as a selection rule beyond that. Better transfer will more likely come from harmonizing recordings and checking that labels and pipelines match than from choosing features.

1Why this question

Speech carries early signs of cognitive decline, but labelled recordings are scarce. Reusing a model trained on a large cohort would save a small study from starting over.

In this project we extract features from conversations: what people say (vocabulary, sentence structure), when they say it (pauses, turn-taking), and how they sound (pitch, voice quality). A model trained on the 90+ cohort (868 labelled participants) could help I-CONECT (156 participants) if its learned patterns carry over.

They may not, because the two cohorts differ in many ways: the kind of conversation, the speech recognition system, the recording setup and the age range. A feature that behaves one way in one cohort can behave differently in the other.

Hypothesis. Features that look alike across cohorts should transfer better. We tested it by sweeping how strict the similarity filter is, from “keep everything” to “keep only near-identical features”.

3How different are the two cohorts?

Taken at face value, half of the features differ strongly between the cohorts. Once each cohort is rescaled on its own, about two thirds have the same shape.

Much of that gap is location and scale, not shape. We rescale each cohort using its own median and spread, then ask how closely the two distributions match, using a score called Q-Q CCC (1 = identical shape). This score is what we later use to filter features.

Technical detail

Q-Q CCC is Lin’s concordance correlation between the 5th–95th percentiles of the two cohorts’ robust-z-scored distributions (z = (x − median) / IQR, computed within each cohort). Median KS statistic falls from 0.44 on the raw scale to 0.09 after rescaling for all features, but only from 0.45 to 0.40 for linguistic features. I-CONECT’s durations are bimodal: count-like linguistic features shift only in the long group, while ratio measures such as MTLD barely shift. Rhythm features (pause ratio, turns per minute) differ in both groups, independent of length.

4How we tested it

We trained on 90+ using only features above a chosen similarity score, tested on I-CONECT, and compared against several baselines that show how much of any gain is real.

  • Similarity threshold. 12 settings from “all 547 features” to “only the 36 features with CCC ≥ 0.995”.
  • Two models. A boosted-tree model (SGBT) and a regularized logistic regression (Elastic Net), so the result does not depend on one model type.
  • Accuracy measure. AUC: the chance that the model scores a random MCI participant higher than a random normal one. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect.

Technical detail

Subject-level mean features; each cohort robust-z-scored with its own median and IQR; transfer = single fit on all of one cohort, 95% bootstrap CI on the target. Within-cohort CV is StratifiedGroupKFold(5) × 10 seeds with normalization fit on the training fold. Random-subset control: 200 draws per threshold, both family-stratified and unstratified. Permutation test: 5,000 label permutations. Paired bootstrap: 2,000 resamples of the same I-CONECT participants across all subsets.

5Does filtering by similarity help?

A little at moderate settings, not at strict ones, and not reliably compared with using every feature.

  • Boosted trees (SGBT) gain up to +0.033 at moderate thresholds (CCC ≥ 0.50–0.85), then fall back to the baseline.
  • Elastic Net drifts up to 0.63 at ≥ 0.98, but the curve is erratic, not a clean trend.
  • Accuracy within 90+ falls steadily as features are removed. Every filter step costs accuracy on the cohort the model learned from.
  • The I-CONECT model, trained and tested on its own data, is at chance. Transfer from 90+ is better than this, so borrowing from the larger cohort does help. The question is only which features to use.

Is the gain more than luck?

Where the SGBT gain comes from. The 0.50 cut removes only 17 features: 16 delta-MFCC means (acoustic measures that mostly track recording conditions) and one interaction feature. That alone gives more than half of the gain (+0.019 of +0.033). The benefit comes from removing a few badly shifted features, not from keeping only highly similar ones.

Compared directly with using all features, no threshold is significantly better (tested on the seven thresholds up to 0.95; largest: SGBT at CCC ≥ 0.70, ΔAUC +0.033, 95% CI −0.004 to +0.071, p = 0.08). Strict thresholds (≥ 0.99) hurt both source accuracy and transfer: distribution similarity is not the same as diagnostic information.

6What do the models rely on?

To see why, we compared the weight each model gives to each feature, for a model trained on 90+ and one trained on I-CONECT.

Start with this caveat. The model trained on I-CONECT has no real signal to weight: its accuracy on held-out I-CONECT participants is 0.48–0.52. Its weights are mostly noise, so a difference between the two models mainly means that I-CONECT’s model has not recovered the 90+ pattern, not that I-CONECT has its own distinct markers. Even a 156-person sample of 90+ reaches 0.60, so small sample size alone does not explain it.

  • Two timing features agree in both cohorts: more interviewer turns and a longer median response delay point towards MCI in both.
  • Several MFCC acoustic features flip sign. For example mfcc_8_mean_std is negative on 90+ and the largest positive weight on I-CONECT.
  • The 90+ model’s strongest feature cannot be used on I-CONECT. ix_interruption_rate (how often speakers overlap) is exactly 0 for every I-CONECT participant, most likely because the turn timestamps never overlap, so it cannot carry any signal there (see section 9).

The shared signal, and what hurts transfer

A model-free view helps: for each feature, does it point the same direction (higher or lower in MCI) in both cohorts? Among features with a clear effect on 90+, direction agreement rises with effect size, and it is perfect for the strongest ones.

The worst case is mfcc_d5_mean_mean: univariate AUC 0.553 on 90+ but 0.391 on I-CONECT. Its similarity score is a respectable 0.66, so a similarity filter at CCC ≥ 0.50 keeps it. Across all features, transfer harm is uncorrelated with the similarity score (ρ = 0.04 for SGBT, −0.05 for Elastic Net), and none of the 20 most harmful features falls below 0.50. A few features that a similarity filter drops are actually helpful to the linear model, possibly because they offset recording differences.

7What this means

  • Do not select features by distribution similarity. It captures whether the numbers look alike, not whether a feature relates to MCI in the same way in both cohorts.
  • A light cleaning step is reasonable. Exclude the few features with very low similarity (CCC < 0.5). The gain is small and not proven against using everything.
  • The shared signal is behavioural. Fluency, pausing and turn-taking carry across cohorts. Many acoustic features do not, and they are the likeliest to mislead a transferred model.
  • A larger cohort helps a smaller one, even imperfectly. A 156-person study alone could not learn the signal, while the 90+ model reaches about 0.6 on it.

8Limits and next steps

Limits

  • One I-CONECT sample of 156. Every transfer interval is about ±0.09 wide, and the random-subset control varies the features, not the people.
  • Many comparisons (12 thresholds × 2 models × several tests); thresholds were not pre-registered and p-values are not corrected.
  • Similarity scores were computed on the same two cohorts used for evaluation (labels were not used).
  • Training sizes differ (868 vs. 156), which confounds comparisons between directions.
  • Individual feature weights are noisy (changes of about ±0.01–0.02 AUC with no interval). Read family-level patterns, not single ranks. Choosing features by direction flips would use I-CONECT labels and leak them.

Next steps

  • Transfer using timing and linguistic features only: a rule that needs no target labels.
  • Test the “drop CCC < 0.5” cleaning rule on another cohort pair, or after matching the speech recognition.
  • Combine with harmonization or domain adaptation instead of selecting features alone.
  • Add confidence intervals to per-feature transfer effects.

9What would help from you

The data-specific questions below would help us tell real cohort differences from differences in how the data were produced. Any one answer is useful.

  1. How was MCI determined? The instrument, cut-offs, and timing relative to the recording, in each cohort, so we can check whether the labels are comparable.
  2. Can overlapping speech be recovered in I-CONECT? The interruption rate is exactly 0 for every participant, which suggests the turn timestamps cannot represent overlap. This is the 90+ model’s single strongest feature, and it is unusable on I-CONECT as things stand.
  3. What determines conversation length? I-CONECT sessions fall into two groups (about 7 and 25 minutes), and many linguistic features differ only in the long group. Knowing the protocol would tell us whether to analyse them separately.
  4. Can the missing audio be located? Acoustic features exist for 86 of 156 participants. More audio, or re-transcription with the same speech recognition system used for 90+, would remove one source of difference.
  5. Is a second sample available? A fresh set of participants would let us test the conclusions without reusing the same 156 people.

Glossary

MCI
Mild cognitive impairment: cognitive decline beyond normal ageing that does not yet meet the threshold for dementia.
AUC
Area under the ROC curve. The chance that the model scores a random MCI participant higher than a random normal one. 0.5 = chance, 1.0 = perfect.
Transfer
Training a model on one cohort and applying it, unchanged, to another.
Within-cohort cross-validation
Training and testing inside one cohort on different participants. It tells us how much signal is learnable there.
Cohen’s d
Difference between two averages divided by their spread. 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.
Robust z-score
A feature minus its median, divided by its interquartile range, computed separately for each cohort. It removes differences in level and scale.
Q-Q CCC
A 0–1 score for how closely two distributions match after rescaling (1 = identical shape).
SGBT / Elastic Net
A gradient-boosted tree model, and a logistic regression that shrinks unhelpful weights towards zero.
Permutation importance
How much accuracy drops when one feature’s values are shuffled. A bigger drop means the model relies on it more.
MFCC
Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients: compact summaries of the sound spectrum, sensitive to microphone and recording conditions.
Bootstrap resample
Re-drawing participants with replacement to estimate how much a result would vary.
